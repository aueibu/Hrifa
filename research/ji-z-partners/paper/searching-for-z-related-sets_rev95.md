# Searching for Z-Related Sets in Just Intonation

A working timeline of methods and results · Nov 22, 2026 · @SecondAB

## Overview

Between 20 and 22 November 2026 the search for just-intonation Z-pairs went from enumerating 12.6 billion subsets of one fixed 5-limit gamut to complete lists in 5-, 7-, 11-, 13- and 17-limit, each finished in seconds to minutes. Every gain came from a piece of mathematics that let the search skip work it could prove useless, checked against an exhaustive list before it was trusted.

A **JI Z-pair** is two octave-reduced pitch sets with the same multiset of intervals, where neither set is a transposition or an inversion of the other. Such pairs do not exist below 6 notes.

The guiding principle, chosen during this work, is the **intervallic odd limit q**: a set qualifies at q when every interval inside it has odd limit at most q. The odd limit of a ratio is the larger of its numerator and denominator after factors of 2 are removed (7/5 has odd limit 7, 45/32 has 45). A low q means a set whose every pair of notes forms a simple ratio.

This document records, in order, the methods used, the mathematics each one rests on, what each found, and how each was checked. It is a working basis for a paper, not the paper itself.

## Mathematical background

Every method below works on sets written as polynomials, and every Z-pair is a product with one factor turned over.

**The lattice.** Ignoring octaves, a ratio 3^a 5^b 7^c ... is the point (a, b, c, ...) in the integer lattice, one axis per odd prime. A pitch set is a finite set of points; an interval is the difference of two points. Transposition is translation; inversion is negation.

**Sets as polynomials.** A set S becomes the Laurent polynomial below, one term per note, with x = (x\_3, x\_5, x\_7, ...).

```latex
P_S(x) = \sum_{v \in S} x^{v}, \qquad P^{*}(x) = P(x^{-1})
```

**Interval content.** The product P(x)·P\*(x) has the coefficient of x^d equal to the number of pairs of notes whose difference is d. So two sets have the same interval content exactly when P\_S P\_S\* = P\_T P\_T\*.

**The factor law** (Rosenblatt and Seymour, "The Structure of Homometric Sets", SIAM J. Alg. Disc. Meth. 3, 1982, Theorems 2.3 and 2.4). In the integer lattice, two sets have equal interval content exactly when their polynomials are products of the same factors with some factors reversed, up to a monomial and a sign. Every Z-pair therefore has the form

```latex
P = U \cdot V, \qquad Q = U \cdot V^{*}, \qquad V \neq V^{*} \text{ (up to a monomial)}
```

where both P and Q have every coefficient 0 or 1. U and V may have negative coefficients, provided they cancel in both products.

**Factor shapes.** A factor V is described by its shape vp,vm: vp terms with coefficient +1 (one placed at the origin) and vm terms with coefficient −1. Two facts restrict the shapes (proved in THEORY\_SMALL\_FACTOR.md):

- **Corners.** Every vertex of the convex hull of V's points has coefficient +1 or −1, and the same holds for U. A −1 term therefore lies inside the hull of the +1 terms, where other contributions can cancel it.
- **Weight.** Evaluating at x = 1 gives k = U(1)·V(1) for a k-note set. A plain (all-plus) factor has V(1) dividing k and V(1) ≥ 3. So at 7, 11 and 13 notes no small plain factor exists, and the small factor must be signed.

**Newton polytopes** (Ostrowski). The hull of a product is the Minkowski sum of the hulls: hull(U·V) = hull(U) + hull(V). In every direction the width of V is at most the width of P. Every method from the product search onward uses this to bound the factors.

**Intervallic odd limit as a region.** Write w\_p = log p. An interval d is allowed at q when both conditions hold:

```latex
\sum_p w_p \max(d_p, 0) \le \log q, \qquad \sum_p w_p \max(-d_p, 0) \le \log q
```

Each left side is a convex function of d, so the allowed intervals D are exactly the lattice points of a convex region R. This fact became central in the 11-limit work (Phase 10).

## Timeline of methods

The search moved from listing subsets, to building pairs from the factor law, to bounding those factors by geometry alone. Each phase gives the method, the mathematics, the result and the check.

### Phase 1 · Exhaustive enumeration (archive analyzed 20 Nov)

- **Method.** An earlier cloud run enumerated every 1–16-note subset of a fixed 5-limit gamut (the 35 pitches of odd limit ≤ 123), about 12.6 billion subsets, and grouped them by interval content.
- **Result.** 30,065 Z-families. Counts spike at 9, 12, 15 and 16 notes (15 notes: 13,364) and stay near zero at 13 notes (49).
- **Finding.** Every one of the 30,065 families factors as P = U·V, Q = U·V\* with exactly one irreducible factor flipped. In 87% (26,036) both factors are plain note sets: P is a "chord of chords" A⊕B and the partner is A⊕(−B). The other 13% use one factor with a −1 coefficient.
- **Check.** An independent brute force reproduced the set-class counts and family lists for every k from 2 to 10; restricted to odd limit ≤ 75 the families matched earlier published counts exactly.
- **Limit.** The gamut fixes which notes may appear, so the result depends on an arbitrary window, and the cost grows with the number of subsets.

### Phase 2 · Cancellation types and placement (20–21 Nov)

- **Method.** `zgen.py types` enumerated the cancellation patterns for each shape symbolically (a U of a notes times a V with b plus and c minus terms), reducing them to geometric types; `zgen.py place` then laid each type into a gamut.
- **Result.** 10 notes, 5-limit: 847 types, 294 families placed.
- **Check.** A fresh exhaustive run (34 min, 73,256,474 set classes) found 295; all 294 placed families are among them with identical members. The one miss needed a mechanism the shapes did not cover (both factors signed).
- **Limit.** At 11, 13 and 14 notes the type searches ran 10.5 hours on 16 cores without finishing. Partial results were recovered from the running processes: 11 notes 25 of 25, 13 notes 1 of 49, 14 notes 305 of 498.

### Phase 3 · Partners by factoring one set (21 Nov)

- **Method.** `zfactor.py` applies the Rosenblatt–Seymour theorem directly: factor a set's polynomial over the integers (sympy), then reverse every subset of its non-symmetric irreducible factors. Each product with 0/1 coefficients is a partner.
- **Result.** Recovered all 295 ten-note families in 17 s, and proved that some sets have no partner anywhere in the lattice (Ptolemy's intense diatonic, the 5-limit chromatic scale).
- **Use.** It answers "does this set have a partner?" exactly, but it starts from a set; it cannot list all pairs.

A side thread the same day carried the JI families into equal divisions of the octave and into harmonic-series rings (linear-ring Z-pairs among re-rooted modes). Those results are recorded in LINEAR\_RING.md and are outside this timeline.

### Phase 4 · Building pairs as products (21 Nov)

- **Method.** `zprod.py` turns the factor law into a generator. It enumerates small signed factors V by shape, then for each V runs a depth-first search over U, point by point in lexicographic order. A coefficient of P or Q is final once its last possible contributor is decided, so any coefficient outside {0, 1} prunes the branch at once.
- **Math.** P = U·V and Q = U·V\* are checked together; Q needs no separate search. A −1 term of V must lie inside the hull of its +1 terms (Corners fact).
- **Result.** A first version with V on a line found only 32 of 295 ten-note families: most families need a two-dimensional flipped factor, such as (x² − xy + y² + 1). With shapes 2,1 3,1 3,2 4,1 4,2 (9,779,902 factors) and U coefficients 0/1/2 it found 294 / 25 / 49 / 496 families at 10 / 11 / 13 / 14 notes. Allowing −1 in U (bounded by `--max-neg`) closed the gap.
- **Check.** Against the original exhaustive archive: 295/295, 25/25, 49/49 and 498/498, nothing extra, in about 9 minutes on 8 cores.

### Phase 5 · Hull pruning (21 Nov)

- **Math.** By Newton polytopes, hull(P) = hull(U) + hull(V), so a factor that cannot fit inside the gamut's hull after some translation never yields a product in the gamut.
- **Result.** 5-limit factors fell from 9.78 million to 615 thousand with identical results (124 s). This made 3-D (7-limit) runs practical.
- **Check.** Against exhaustive 7-limit counts at odd limits 27, 63 and 75 (for example odd 75: 20, 2 and 8 families at 6, 7 and 8 notes): all matched.

### Phase 6 · The intervallic odd limit (21 Nov)

- **Decision.** The guiding principle became the intervallic odd limit q: every interval inside a set, not just every note against 1/1, must have odd limit ≤ q.
- **Method.** `--interval-odd q`: each P note that becomes final is checked against all earlier final notes, and the branch is pruned when a difference leaves the allowed set D. Q has the same intervals as P, so it needs no check.
- **Check.** With q = 729 inside the odd ≤ 123 gamut, the run matched the exhaustive catalog family for family at every size checked, with no interval above q.
- **Finding.** Scored this way, simplicity and gamut size separate: a pair can use notes far from 1/1 and still have only simple intervals.

### Phase 7 · No gamut: the width argument (21 Nov)

- **Math.** Every difference of two notes of P is an allowed interval, so P's width in any direction is at most the largest width of D. By Newton polytopes V's width is at most P's. Hence every difference of two points of V lies in conv(D), and likewise for U. Separately, the lexicographic minimum of a product is the sum of the factors' minima, so fixing U's lowest point at the origin with coefficient 1 builds each set once instead of at every position.
- **Method.** `--fast`: no gamut at all; factors are pruned by D alone and U is anchored at 0.
- **Result.** 5-limit q ≤ 729: factors fell from about 76 million to about 188 thousand. The slow run (64 min on 14 cores) and the fast run (8 min on 2 cores) agreed family for family.
- **Finding.** The complete 5-limit list for q ≤ 729 has 2,376 families, exactly the old catalog's families with q ≤ 729. Every such pair already fits inside the odd ≤ 123 window, so that catalog was complete up to q = 729. The last 17 families needed a plain 3-note chord flipped against a U with one or two −1 coefficients, which fixed the recipe ("the ladder": main shapes, 5-term shapes, 3,0 with signed U, sum-1 shapes one size at a time).

### Phase 8 · The 7-limit ladder (22 Nov)

- **Run.** The same recipe for primes 3, 5, 7 and q ≤ 243: 70 minutes on 16 cores.
- **Result.** 6,233 families. The simplest is q = 45 at 9 notes, for example 1 7/6 6/5 4/3 7/5 3/2 5/3 9/5 28/15 \~ 1 9/8 7/6 5/4 7/5 3/2 5/3 7/4 9/5. No exhaustive 7-limit list exists at this q, so completeness rests on the factor law plus the 5-limit validation of the recipe.

### Phase 9 · How small can the flipped factor be? (22 Nov)

- **Math.** The Weight and Corners facts (Mathematical background) rule out a universal 3-note chord: it needs 3 to divide k.
- **Conjecture S.** For 5-limit q ≤ 729, every family has a factorization using only shape 3,0 with U allowed up to two −1 coefficients, or shapes 3,1, 4,0 and 4,1 with plain U. True for all 2,376 families.
- **Test.** On the 7-limit ladder these shapes found every family except one 16-note family, a plain 4-note chord times a 6-term factor with one −1.
- **Widened form S'.** Allowing a signed U with shape 4,0 as well finds all 6,233 7-limit families, and later all 254 11-limit families with q ≤ 99, in a fraction of the full ladder's time (2 min against 30). It remains a conjecture.

### Phase 10 · The 11-limit and the hull that was never needed (22 Nov)

- **Problem.** The fast search used the lattice points of conv(D). Computing them by checking every group of 4 points for a facet costs about n⁵/24 operations: 10 minutes at 11-limit q = 63, 19 minutes at q = 99, over 50 minutes at q = 135, paid again in every step.
- **First fixes.** Caching the hull per (primes, q); then an exact vertex-refinement method (find facets on a few certain vertices, add any point outside, repeat): q = 135 in 0.4 s. In 5 dimensions (13-limit) it slowed again to 109 s.
- **Proof.** The allowed set D is the set of lattice points of the convex region R (Mathematical background). conv(D) lies inside R, so every lattice point of conv(D) is already in D. The hull computation returns D itself, for any primes and any q.
- **Check.** Equal in all 19 cases tested: the four cached 11-limit hulls, 14 sets of 2 to 4 primes by brute force (q from 15 to 2,025), and a 13-limit set by the vertex method. A 13-limit count went from 914 s to 8 s.
- **Result.** 11-limit q ≤ 99 (30 min): 254 families; 11 first appears at q = 55, and the simplest 12- and 15-note pairs drop from q 75 and 135 to 55 and 77.

### Phase 11 · A compiled search (22 Nov)

- **Measurement first.** On the 11-limit q ≤ 99 run, the U search itself took under 4 minutes; the rest was the hull (now gone) and factor generation on one core.
- **Method.** `zprod-rs`, a Rust port of factor generation and the U search, with the Python code kept for grouping products into families so the output format did not change.
- **Obstacle.** The first versions were correct but no faster: with 16 threads, generation took 88 s against 37 s on one, with 431 s of system time, because the threads competed for memory allocation inside the exact hull test for minus points. Moving that work onto fixed-size stack arrays, a precomputed table of compatible point pairs, and reusable per-thread buffers cut generation to 3.4 s.
- **Result.** 11-limit q ≤ 99 ladder: 30 minutes → about 1 minute (main shapes 227 s → 4.7 s). 7-limit q ≤ 243: 70 minutes → 130 s. 5-limit q ≤ 729: 30 s.
- **Check.** Identical families to the Python runs at 5-, 7- and 11-limit, and the 5-limit list again equals the exhaustive catalog.

### Phase 12 · Higher primes (22 Nov)

- **Runs.** 13-limit q ≤ 99 (3 min), 17-limit q ≤ 85 (2 min), 11-limit q ≤ 135 (3 min), plus 13-limit q ≤ 65 and 17-limit q ≤ 51 earlier in Python.
- **Result.** 13 first appears at q = 65 and gives the first 16-note pairs (q = 91); 17 first appears at q = 85. Counts are in Results.

### Phase 13 · Shape-free enumeration: the lists become complete (23 Nov)

- **Math.** A set qualifies at q exactly when every pairwise difference is an allowed interval. With its lowest note fixed at the origin, its other notes are lexicographically positive allowed intervals that are pairwise compatible: a clique in a fixed graph on those intervals. Enumerating every clique assumes nothing about factors.
- **Method.** `zprod-rs --enum`: bitset clique enumeration, with each set's interval content hashed as it grows (a sum of random keys per difference, two independent 64-bit hashes). A set and its inversion always share their content, so only one of the two is kept. Hash slices bound the memory; sets with a repeated hash are rechecked exactly (full difference lists, canonical forms).
- **Counts first.** Sets of 6 to 16 notes: about 59 million (5-limit q ≤ 729), 4.0 billion (7-limit q ≤ 243), 2.9 billion (11-limit q ≤ 135), 0.42 billion (13-limit q ≤ 99) and 0.15 billion (17-limit q ≤ 85); each count took under 3 seconds.
- **Result.** All five enumerations (7 s, 261 s, 147 s, 23 s and 9 s on 16 cores) found exactly the families of the factor search, at every size, with nothing missing in either direction; every family verified. The five lists are therefore complete without any assumption about factor shapes, and the amended form of Conjecture S' holds throughout these ranges.

### Phase 14 · Highest q for every limit, and where the factor recipe breaks (2026-11-23)

- **Shape census** on the five complete lists: every family has a flipped factor of at most 5 terms, all of the amended S' shapes; only 3-term factors up to q 75 in every limit, 4-term from about q 99, 5-term only at 7-limit above q 135 and 5-limit above q 405.
- **Faster enumerator** (hybrid): 17-limit q ≤ 135 in 1,065 s (v2: 1,302 s), identical lists. Bitsets widened to 2,048 vertices, validated on 5-limit q 729 and 7-limit q 243.
- **Overnight push**: the Rust ladder for reach, shape-free enumeration underneath as proof. Two corrections to the ladder were needed:
  1. **Node budget.** The ladder stops a factor's search after 2 million nodes. At q ≥ 243 these stops hid real families (11-limit q 243: 8; 13-limit q 243: 51; 7-limit q 405: 83). Every rung now reruns its stopped steps with no budget.
  2. **Signed U.** At 5-limit q 2,025–2,187 the enumeration found 6 families the ladder missed with no budget stops: 5 are a plain 3-note chord flipped with U needing three −1 coefficients (the recipe allowed two), 1 is a 3,1 factor with U needing two −1 (the recipe used plain U). At q 3,645 the widened ladder still missed 25: plain 4- and 5-note chords with U needing two or three −1. At 7-limit q 729 the widened steps add 4,435 families.
- **Conclusion:** as q grows, the flipped factor V stays a small plain chord; what grows is the negative mass of U. Ladder counts above the proven ranges are lower bounds. Exact factorization of every proven family then showed why: each set's polynomial is (almost always) two irreducible factors, one a plain chord, and U is just the other factor.

### Phase 15 · Three constructions explain the proven lists (2026-11-23 evening)

- **Chord sums.** Conjecture T in plain form: P = A·U, Q = A⁻¹·U with A a small chord and U a plain set. An exact test on all nine lists (every grouping of the irreducible factors) finds 85.4% to 99.5% of families of this form; a generator rebuilds exactly those families on every list, and found one family the 11-limit q 405 ladder rung missed.
- **Holes and merges.** Every other family is a stack with subtracted copies: either whole chord copies cut out (holes) or overlapping copies whose doubled notes merge into one pitch (merges). A hole generator and the Rust factor search with small signed factors each rebuild their families exactly.
- **Coverage.** Together the constructions build every family of the seven 7- to 19-limit lists and of 5-limit q ≤ 729, and all but 1 and 9 at 5-limit q ≤ 2,187 and 3,645. See "Structure: three constructions".

### Phase 16 · Small sets, 6 to 9 notes, limits 5 to 23 (2026-11-24 overnight)

- **Enumeration by size.** The shape-free enumerator run for 6, 7, 8 and 9 notes only. Its cost is set by the largest size in a run, so small sizes reach far higher q than the 6 to 16-note lists: 5-limit to q 177,147 (6 notes) and q 98,415 (7 to 9 notes), 7-limit to q 10,935 (6 and 7 notes).
- **23-limit added.** The first 23-limit lists, proven to q 243 for 7 to 9 notes and q 405 for 6 notes.
- **Scheduling.** Runs went one at a time with a time cap; a step whose estimated cost (the step below it times about 15 to 20) could not fit its cap was skipped. 13-limit q 405 for 7 to 9 notes did not finish in 75 minutes or in a 2-hour retry. A run counts as proven only when its result file was written: the wrapper reports success even after a crash or a kill.
- **Cross-check.** Where the factor-search ladder reached the same range it found the same families, one for one. See "Small sets: 6 to 9 notes".

### Phase 17 · 6 to 8 notes, one size per run (2026-11-25 overnight)

- **Forecast, then run.** Each run is one limit, one q and one note count. A count-only pass gives the exact number of sets to check in seconds to minutes, and the run time follows from the measured speed, so no run works against a time cap. Runs with large top-note groups took 0.27 to 0.6 times their forecast.
- **Resumable.** The enumerator saves after every batch; a stopped run continues where it left off and gives the same list. A Spot preemption at 06:53 Eastern cost one minute.
- **6 to 8 notes first.** For composition the 6- to 8-note sets matter most, so the night went to them; the 9-note steps (13-limit q 405, 7-limit q 2,187) wait for a later run. 31 lists finished and every family was checked; 13-limit 7 notes at q 1,215 stopped a third of the way and will resume. Lists: `data/proven/n25_runs_2026-11-25.tar.xz`, manifest `data/manifest_2026-11-25.json`.

### Phase 18 · Shapes and universal types (2026-11-25 evening)

- **Shapes.** Every proven 6- to 8-note family (810 across limits 5 to 23) is a linear image of a few fixed patterns placed in the lattice. A builder that places those patterns reproduced all 21 proven lists family for family.
- **Universal types.** A Z-pair's interval matching gives linear equations that are the same on every prime axis, so each matching defines one pattern, and every pair with that matching (any limit, any q) is an integer image of it. There are finitely many matchings per note count, so finitely many patterns.
- **Complete search.** A search over all matchings, written in Python and then in Rust, settled 6 notes (one pattern, 5.0 M search nodes) and 7 notes (one 2-D pattern and five 1-D ones, 1.89 G nodes, 38 minutes on 64 cores). See "Universal types" under Small sets.

**Why 3-limit reaches so far.** In 3-limit every note is a power of 3, so a set is a set of positions on the chain of fifths: the lattice has one dimension instead of two or more. q is 3^span (span in fifths), so each extra fifth multiplies q by 3 while the number of sets only doubles (2^(span−1) sets of a given span). q ≤ 3²⁰ is about a million sets and took 10 s in plain Python (`enum3.py`); 5-limit q ≤ 3,645 was 28 billion sets. The other side of this: 3-limit pairs need a wide span, so none exist below q = 3¹¹ = 177,147 (span 11: one 6-note and one 9-note family). ### Universal types: 6 and 7 notes classified

Every 6-note Z-pair, at any prime limit and any q, is an integer copy of one pattern (zgen type 1). Every 7-note Z-pair is an integer copy of one 2-D pattern (zgen type 19) or of one of five 1-D patterns, four of them new. Both results are computer-assisted: they rest on the search code being correct.

**Why finitely many patterns.** In a Z-pair (P, Q) each interval of P equals plus or minus an interval of Q. Fixing that matching gives linear equations that are the same on every prime axis, so every pair with that matching, in any lattice, is an integer linear image of one generic pattern. There are finitely many matchings for a given note count, so there are finitely many patterns, independent of primes and q.

**The search.** It walks the matchings as flats of a hyperplane arrangement. It drops a flat once it forces two notes to coincide or Q to be a shifted or mirrored P, and it merges flats that differ only by relabeling, mirroring or swapping the two sets. Every maximal flat that still carries a full matching is a pattern. The integer points of each pattern form a lattice; the maps found to the zgen types are integer matrices with determinant ±1 in both directions, so "copy" holds over the integers.

### Which q values the types reach

A type placed in the lattice has q equal to the largest odd limit among its interval vectors, which fixes where every family can appear. Place a type's points at x·a + y·b, where a and b are ratios (octaves ignored). Each interval of the type is then a^x b^y for an integer vector (x, y), so

```latex
q(a,b) = \max_{(x,y)\in V} \operatorname{odd}\left(a^{x} b^{y}\right)
```

where V is the type's set of interval vectors and the notes must stay distinct. The 6-note type (zgen type 1) has 15 vectors: (0,1) (0,2) (1,-3) (1,-2) (1,-1) (1,0) (1,1) (1,2) (2,-3) (2,-2) (2,-1) (2,0) (2,1) (3,-2) (3,-1). Taking a = 5 and b = 3, the vector (3,-2) gives 125/9, so q = 125 = 5³; this is why a new prime p first appears at p³. ## Structure: three constructions

Every family in the nine proven lists except 9 is built by one of three explicit constructions, and each construction has a generator that reproduces its share of every list exactly. All three are the factor law with one factor kept plain: P = T·S and the partner T·S\* (or T\*·S), with T a plain set and S a small factor whose coefficients are +1 and at most four −1.

Notation follows the Harmonic Field Lab: a chord is written a:b:c (otonal) and its mirror 1/(a:b:c) (utonal). For a set A, A⁻¹ = {1/r : r in A} is its mirror, and A·U = {a·u} is the product set.

### Chord sums: A·U and A⁻¹·U

Take a small chord A and any set U, and multiply every note of A by every note of U, with no product landing twice. Doing the same with the mirrored chord gives the Z-partner. Example (19-limit list, 9 notes, q 45): A = 4:5:6 = {1, 5/4, 3/2}, U = {1, 9/8, 7/4}. A·U = {1, 35/32, 9/8, 5/4, 21/16, 45/32, 3/2, 27/16, 7/4} and A⁻¹·U = {1, 9/8, 7/6, 4/3, 7/5, 3/2, 8/5, 7/4, 9/5}.

- The intervals of A·U are the products of an interval of A with an interval of U, and A⁻¹ has the same intervals as A. So both sets pass the odd-limit test together, and a product lands twice in one exactly when it does in the other.
- Chord sums exist only at 9, 12, 15 and 16 notes (factor sizes 3×3, 3×4, 3×5, 4×4). A 2-note set is its own mirror, and a mirror-symmetric factor makes the partner a plain mirror of P.
- They are 85.4% of the 5-limit q ≤ 3,645 list, 98.1% of 7-limit q ≤ 315 and 99.2–99.5% of the 11- to 19-limit q ≤ 135 lists. The share falls with q only at 5-limit (about 93% to q 729, 86–88% above).
- Few chords do the work: the 19-limit list uses 347 (one per family), led by 3:4:5 (the 4:5:6 pitch classes) in 1,042 families, then 5:6:7, 4:5:7, 5:6:9 and 4:6:7 at about 260–275 each.

### Holes and merges

In the remaining families one factor has minus signs: P = T·S+ − T·S−. T is stacked on every note of S+, then one more copy of T is subtracted for each note of S−. In every such family on all nine lists no note of the stack lands more than twice, and every subtracted note lands on a note of the stack.

- **Hole.** A plain 3- to 5-note chord T stacked with no overlaps; each subtracted copy of the chord is already spelled, note for note, by other copies and is cut out whole. Nothing merges. This is every such family at 7- to 19-limit and 95% of them at 5-limit q ≤ 3,645.
- **Merge.** A small signed factor (3 or 4 plus notes, one or two minus) times a larger plain set. Copies overlap, and each doubled note becomes one pitch when the extra copy is subtracted; the rest of that copy removes single notes. All 8-, 10- and 14-note families are merges.

The partner needs no separate check. If P = T·S has coefficients 0 or 1, so does T·S\*: its integer coefficients sum to k and their squares also sum to k (the zero-lag autocorrelation, shared with P), and an integer with c² = c is 0 or 1.

### Generators and coverage

Each construction has a generator, checked family by family against every proven list: it must build exactly the families of its kind, with none missing and none outside the list. All passed.

- **Chord sums** (`chordgen.py`): for each chord A, the allowed intervals d with d·δ allowed for every interval δ of A, and outside A's intervals; every U whose intervals all lie there gives a pair. 19-limit q ≤ 135 in 82 s, against 12,591 s for the enumeration proof.
- **Holes** (`holegen.py`): the hole's chord copy must be spelled by other copies, so the stacking set contains some of the chord's own intervals. The search tries every such choice, then every further note compatible with a fixed surviving note. 5-limit q ≤ 3,645: 5,880 families in 25 s.
- **Merges**: the Rust factor search with shapes 3,1, 4,1 and 4,2, plain second factor and no node budget is exactly this construction (with a plain T, a minus point of S must lie inside the hull of its plus points). 5-limit q ≤ 3,645: 2,453 of 2,453 in 17 s.
- **Wider forms** (5-limit only): a 3- to 5-note chord times a factor with up to four minus signs (several subtracted copies), and the weight-1 shapes 2,1 and 3,2 times a plain set (the 7-, 11- and 13-note families).

### Every family has a kind

Each family in the nine proven lists is given exactly one kind, tested in this order: chord sum, hole, merge, wide, left over. All but 9 families, all at 5-limit q ≤ 3,645, have a kind. A chord sum is P = A·U and Q = A⁻¹·U with A a 3- or 4-note chord (a:b:c, mirror 1/(a:b:c)) and U a plain set. A hole is the same product with one whole copy of the chord cut out. A merge is a small first factor with one minus sign (3 or 4 plus-notes, 1 or 2 minus-notes) times a plain set, where overlapping copies land on the same pitch. Wide covers a chord times a factor with several minus signs (up to 3, and 4 at 5-limit q ≤ 3,645), and the small factors 2,1 and 3,2 that give the prime-size families (7, 11 and 13 notes). Left over means no generator found it; at 5-limit q ≤ 3,645 these 9 have both factors signed or a note counted twice (the residue).

### The residue

Nine proven families (5-limit, 13 to 16 notes, q 2,187–3,645) fit none of the constructions, and more exist above the proven range: a hunt at q ≤ 6,561 finds at least 59. Every one is still exactly two irreducible factors with one flipped, so the two-factor law holds for all of them; only "one factor is a plain set with ±1 partner" fails.

## Limits and open questions

All five lists are proven complete by shape-free enumeration (Phase 13): 5-limit q ≤ 729, 7-limit q ≤ 243, 11-limit q ≤ 135, 13-limit q ≤ 99 and 17-limit q ≤ 85, for 6 to 16 notes. Beyond those ranges (higher q, more notes, other limits) the factor search is the tool, and its completeness rests on the assumption that bounded factor shapes are enough; enumeration cost grows quickly with q.

- **Bounded shapes.** The factor law guarantees a factorization, not a small one. The ladder searches factors of at most 5 plus terms and 2 minus terms, with U coefficients from −1 to 2 under a cap on negatives. Within the five enumerated ranges it provably suffices (the enumeration found nothing else); beyond them no theorem says so.
- **Conjecture S'.** Every family in the complete lists (6 to 16 notes) has a factorization using shapes 3,0 or 4,0 with U allowed up to two −1 coefficients, or 3,1 or 4,1 with plain U. As a universal statement it is false: those shapes have weight 2, 3 or 4, while a 7-note set needs a flipped factor of weight ±1 or ±7, so the 7-note pair at q = 1,215 is a counterexample. A provable form must either exclude prime note counts, add weight-1 shapes such as 2,1, or name a range. Two tools look central for a proof: Rosenblatt–Seymour reduces the flipped factor to a product of non-symmetric irreducible factors, and Mahler measure bounds it (1 < M(V) ≤ M(P) ≤ √k, by multiplicativity, Landau's inequality and the Smyth–Boyd characterization of measure 1). The missing step is a lemma that such a factor can always be replaced by a small one. The shape-free enumeration of Phase 13 proves the amended form for every family in the five ranges; a proof for all q still needs that lemma.
- **S' fails at high q (2026-11-23).** The amended form holds in every range enumerated up to 5-limit q ≈ 2,000, 7-limit q 315 and 11/13/17-limit q 135. At 5-limit q 2,025–3,645 the enumeration finds families whose only small factors need U with three or more −1 coefficients (plain 3-, 4- and 5-note chords) or signed U on 3,1. The flipped factor V stays a small plain chord; what grows with q is the negative mass of U. Exact factorization (sympy, all nine proven lists, about 85,000 distinct families) explains it: a Z-pair's polynomial is almost always a product of exactly two irreducible factors (100% at 11–19-limit q ≤ 135, 98.4% at 5-limit q ≤ 3,645, the rest three or four), the partner reverses one of them, and one factor is almost always a plain chord (a 3-note chord in 97–99% of families at 11–19-limit q ≤ 135). S' fixed V as the small factor and limited U's signs; U is simply the other irreducible factor. Only 7 families (5-limit, q 2,187–3,645) have no plain-chord factor at all. Conjecture T: P = A·B with A, B irreducible and the partner A·B\*, with one of them a plain chord of 3 to 5 notes in almost every case.
- **Are the three constructions complete?** Chord sums, holes and merges build every proven family except 9 (5-limit, q 2,187–3,645). No proof says they must; the proven lists are the test bed, and any family outside them would mark a new kind. Open: why more primes leave less room for holes and merges (5-limit 15% of families, 7-limit 2%, 11- to 19-limit under 1%), and how the residue grows with q.
- **Prime note counts.** 7-note pairs first appear at 5-limit q = 1,215; 11- and 13-note pairs have appeared only at 5-limit, at q ≤ 2,187 (one) and q ≤ 3,645 (three). Is there a lower bound on q for such pairs, or a reason they are rare?
- **First appearance at 5p.** 11, 13 and 17 each first appear at q = 5p. So does 19, at q = 95 (ladder, 2026-11-23).
- **Comma shades.** The comma criterion (under about 22 cents between matched notes) is a listening judgment applied after the search; it is not yet part of the definition.
- **Not taken yet.** 23-limit and higher; proofs above the proven ranges (sets to enumerate: 11-limit q 225 107 B, 13-limit q 165 232 B, 17-limit q 153 302 B, 7-limit q 405 358 B); a ladder with unrestricted signed U; Z-pairs on rings and equal divisions carried over from these lists.

**Tool ceilings (6 to 9-note search).** "All primes" stops at 23-limit: the factor search handles at most 8 odd primes, and the enumerator at most 2,048 allowed intervals (19-limit q 1,215 is already past it). Beyond those, a 9-note step costs 15 to 20 times the step below, so 13-limit q 405 and 17- and 19-limit q 405 are out of reach for one night on 16 cores; the ladder lower bounds above are the best counts there.
