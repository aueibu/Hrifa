Z-Pair Lattice

The pictures behind the search: which intervals are allowed at a given q, what a factor shape like 3,1 looks like, and how one set U times a factor V and its flip V* gives two chords with the same intervals.

1 · The allowed intervals

Every allowed interval is a lattice point, and the hull adds none

Each interval is a point: steps along one prime to the right, along the other prime up (octaves ignored). An interval is allowed when its numerator and denominator, with factors of 2 removed, are both at most q. On a log scale those two conditions are straight-edged, so they cut out a convex shape (dashed). Its lattice points are exactly the allowed intervals, which is why the search never needs to compute a hull.

allowed interval not allowed hull of the allowed points the convex limit shape

This is a two-prime slice: the other primes' exponents are 0. With more primes the shape gains a dimension per prime (11-limit is 4-D), and the same argument holds in every dimension.

2 · Factor shapes

What “3,1” means

A factor V is a small pattern of points with signs. The shape vp,vm counts them: vp plus points (one sits at the origin) and vm minus points. A minus point must lie inside the plus points' outline, because something has to cancel it. The flip V* is V turned half a revolution. A factor that equals its own flip is useless (it gives P = Q), so it is skipped.

3 · Building a pair

P = U · V and Q = U · V*

Place a copy of V at every point of U and add the coefficients up; that is P. Do the same with the flip V* to get Q. When every coefficient in both comes out 0 or 1, P and Q are two sets of notes with exactly the same intervals: a Z-pair (unless Q is just P moved or turned over). Click the grids to edit. In U a click cycles 0 → 1 → 2 → −1; in V it cycles 0 → +1 → −1.
